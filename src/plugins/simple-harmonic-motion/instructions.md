# Simple Harmonic Motion

Investigate a mass hanging from a spring. Compare two experimental methods for estimating the spring constant, **k**, and watch how mass affects the motion.

## The static method

When the mass is motionless, the spring force balances its weight:

**mg = kx**

Rearranging gives:

**k = mg / x**

Here, **m** is mass in kilograms, **g** is gravitational acceleration, and **x** is the spring's extension from its natural length.

Record measurements for several masses. A graph of force **mg** against extension **x** should be approximately linear, with slope **k**.

## The dynamic method

When the mass oscillates with a small amplitude, its period is approximately:

**T = 2π√(m / k)**

Squaring both sides gives a linear relationship:

**T² = (4π² / k)m**

Measure the time for several oscillations and divide by the number of oscillations to find **T**. Timing multiple oscillations reduces the effect of reaction-time error.

The simulated spring has a small mass of its own, so the dynamic estimate may differ slightly from the static estimate. That difference is part of the experiment.

## Reading the simulation

- The graph shows spring extension over time.
- Each color represents a different mass that you have tested.
- The dashed reference marks the current equilibrium position.
- The spring oscillates around equilibrium rather than around its natural length.
- The statistics below the visualization show the active method, mass, measured quantity, and number of data rows.

## Explore

Try several masses with both methods. As you collect data, consider:

- How does increasing mass change the equilibrium extension?
- How does increasing mass change the period?
- Do the static and dynamic methods produce the same value of **k**?
- Why is a small oscillation amplitude important for this model?
